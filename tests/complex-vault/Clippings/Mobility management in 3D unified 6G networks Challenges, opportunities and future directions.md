## 1\. Introduction

Six-generation (6G) wireless networks are poised to revolutionize the communication landscape, introducing groundbreaking enhancements over their fifth-generation (5G) predecessors . Data rates are expected to surge to an unprecedented level, reaching 1 terabit per second (Tbps), a significant leap from 5G’s 10 Gbps , , . Latency, the time it takes for data to travel between devices, will be drastically reduced to 0.1 ms, a tenfold improvement over 5G’s 1 ms . 6G will introduce 3D coverage capabilities, providing users with an exceptional experience of 10 Gbps per cubic meter (Gbps/m $3$), compared to 5G’s 50 Mbps per square meter (Mbps/m $2$) . This 3D coverage will enable seamless connectivity even in dense urban environments and remote areas. Localization accuracy will reach 1 cm in 3D space, significantly surpassing 5G’s 10 cm in 2D . This enhanced accuracy will pave the way for precision applications such as autonomous vehicles and augmented reality. To support these ambitious technical specifications, 6G will embrace cutting-edge technologies, including artificial intelligence (AI) and edge and cloud computing . AI will permeate 6G networks, orchestrating operations, managing resource allocation, power control, and enabling seamless handovers , . Edge computing will bring computational capabilities closer to the user, reducing latency and enabling real-time processing , , . Cloud computing will provide the necessary scalability and resilience to support the massive data demands of 6G applications . Furthermore, with the rapid increase in data-centric services, there is growing attention toward producer mobility and content-centric communication, as seen in recent works such as , . These developments align with our vision of intelligent, multi-segment mobility solutions for future 6G networks.

Regarding network architecture, 6G is expected to adopt a cell-free 3D unified network architecture, integrating the terrestrial and non-terrestrial networks (NTNs), making the 3D unified network span the space, air, and ground communication segments , . This will deliver 3D 6G connectivity through the collaboration of satellites in space, aerial systems including unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), high altitude platforms (HAPs), and aircraft together with terrestrial networks . Each network segment has its distinct altitude and mobility characteristics, as shown in . Geostationary (GEO) satellites appear stationary at around 35,786 kilometers (km) above the earth’s surface in the space segment. Medium earth orbit (MEO) satellites operate at moderate speeds in orbits ranging from 300 to 2000 km, while low earth orbit (LEO) satellites move at high speeds in orbits of ranging from 160 to 300 km. In the air segment, HAPs and aircraft follow pre-planned paths at around 50 km altitudes, while UAVs navigate through pre-planned or random trajectories at lower altitudes, ranging from several tens to hundreds of meters. On the ground segment, terrestrial base stations (BSs) and mobile user equipments (UEs) are positioned on the Earth’s surface, either stationary or moving at speeds dependent on the specific application.

In this 3D unified network, mobility management poses a significant challenge and is a critical design consideration. Unlike traditional terrestrial and 5G networks, where BSs are stationary, and UEs are mobile, BSs can fly, maneuver, and operate in constellations in 6G networks , . This necessitates a multi-layered approach to mobility management and dynamic resource allocation , :

- •
	**Space Segment:** Due to their high altitudes ranging between 300 and 2000 Km and moderate speeds of around 1000 m per second (m/s), MEO satellites experience relatively longer visibility times of a few minutes . In contrast, LEO satellites, operating at altitudes ranging from 160 to 300 km and speeds of over 8000 m/s, have significantly shorter visibility windows of only a few seconds . This necessitates frequent and seamless handovers among communication links to maintain continuous connectivity. Additionally, handovers between GEO satellites, MEO satellites, and LEO satellites must be handled as LEO and MEO satellites move within their constellation orbits. The dynamics of the air segment, with flying and maneuvering aircrafts, HAPs, and UAVs, further complicate the issue of LEO and MEO satellite visibility . To address these challenges, novel and highly efficient mobility and resource management approaches are required, considering the mutual velocities and Doppler shifts between different network entities. This is particularly critical when space-to-air connections relay information from ground users or Internet of Things (IoT) devices to the nearest ground base station (GBS) , .
- •
	**Air Segment:** The intermediate network layer, consisting of aircrafts, HAPs, and UAVs, as depicted in , introduces three levels of mobility management. The first level arises from the need for efficient trajectory planning, encompassing altitude and hovering time adjustments to optimize aerials’ coverage within their limited energy resources. The second level stems from the frequency of handovers, dynamic resource allocation, and power control among these mobile aerial entities. The third level emerges from the dynamic selection and handovers of MEO and LEO satellites by the air components, further complicating the mobility management process .
- •
	**Ground Segment:** comprising fixed BSs and UEs such as handheld handsets, vehicles, IoT devices, and high-speed trains (HSTs), introduces an additional dimension to mobility management beyond the traditional BS-UE interactions. UEs can seamlessly transition between terrestrial, aerial, and satellite networks in this layer, necessitating robust handover mechanisms and dynamic resource allocation strategies. The terrestrial segment also enables data relaying and multiplexing across different network layers, allowing UEs to offload traffic to other UEs, fixed BSs, aerial entities, or satellites. This capability not only enhances network capacity but also extends coverage and facilitates seamless connectivity in challenging environments. Integrating terrestrial, aerial, and satellite networks in the 3D unified 6G network revolutionizes mobility management and dynamic resource allocation, demanding innovative and adaptive approaches to handle the complexities of these multi-layered 3D networks .

This paper delves into the intricacies of mobility management in the emerging 6G system, characterized by its unified 3D architecture. While significant research efforts have been undertaken within each network segment independently, such as time-varying channel estimation and dynamic resource allocation, handovers in the space and air-to-ground domains, aerial trajectory planning, and dynamic routing across 3D network layers, this paper aims to provide a holistic perspective on this multifaceted challenge. Also, a case study of joint energy-aware UAV trajectory planning (UTP) and UAV-centric LEO satellite handover enabled by online learning is presented to showcase the power of AI in managing mobility across air and space segments. This case study highlights the potential of AI to optimize resource utilization, minimize energy consumption, and ensure seamless connectivity in the complex and dynamic 3D unified 6G network. Finally, the paper explores opportunities and future directions, including the vision of a unified network core and protocol stacks, the use of advanced mobility-aware modulation schemes, and the need for proactive and secure handover approaches.

### 2.1. Time-varying channel estimation and dynamic resource allocation

In 3D unified 6G networks, the mobility of UAVs, satellites, and ground nodes poses significant challenges to time-varying channel estimation and resource allocation. The most dynamic satellite-UAV channel, affected by the Doppler effect and dynamic weather conditions, experiences time-varying shifts and signal attenuation, impacting carrier frequency offset and severely affecting transmission signals. Acquiring channel state information (CSI) becomes challenging due to rapid changes in relative positions and beamforming angles between satellites and UAVs , . Existing channel estimation schemes — blind, semi-blind, and pilot-aided — have limitations in fast time-varying scenarios. Pilot-aided schemes, leveraging known pilot information, show promise but require careful consideration of pilot structure and length. Recent studies explore methods like Bernstein basis polynomials for better estimation in high-mobility channels and 3D message passing for channel tracking. However, critical issues remain: optimizing pilot structures for fast variations, exploring joint channel estimation and decoding designs, and investigating joint pilot structure and channel coding to reduce overheads while enhancing accuracy. Addressing these challenges is pivotal for robust time-varying channel estimation in satellite-UAV communications, ensuring effective communication in highly mobile environments .

Dynamic resource allocation in highly mobile networks, encompassing resource multiplexing and multiple user access, is a pivotal challenge. Within this dynamic environment, adapting resource multiplexing — time, frequency, and space resources — requires constant updates to accommodate the rapidly changing CSI, Doppler shifts, and signal spreading even within a single frame. Techniques like non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA) offer promising solutions, leveraging variability in power and code domains. However, the efficacy of power domain NOMA hinges on precise CSI estimation for successive interference cancellation (SIC) operations, a formidable challenge in this context. Furthermore, the network’s heterogeneity, with varying power requirements across elements, complicates the adoption of power domain NOMA. Code domain NOMA alleviates CSI estimation concerns but introduces synchronization and user limitations. Traditional NOMA schemes demand substantial enhancements to align with the demands of high-mobility 3D unified 6G networks. Advanced approaches, such as the 3D factor graph scheme proposed in , address these challenges through iterative stages — interference mitigation, Doppler compensation, and decoding — tailored for individual user signal reception. Yet, despite its innovation, this scheme grapples with high complexity and time consumption, constraining its performance, especially in high-speed scenarios.

### 2.2. Handover in 3D unified 6G networks

Handover operations are fundamental for wireless networks, allowing seamless transitions of communication links as users or network entities move across coverage areas. In 3D unified 6G networks, which integrate space, air, and ground segments, the handover mechanisms are significantly more complex due to the diverse mobility patterns, altitudes, and operational characteristics of the network entities. This section provides a detailed exploration of handover categories, their associated challenges, innovative management approaches, a practical example, and the role of standardization in 6G networks , , .

#### 2.2.1. Categories of handovers in 6G networks

In 3D unified 6G networks, handovers are classified into intra-segment and inter-segment types, as depicted in .

- •
	**Intra-segment Handovers**: These occur within the same network layer , :
	- –
		**Terrestrial Handovers**: Transitions between ground BSs, common in cellular networks, must now support higher user speeds (e.g., high-speed trains) and diverse UE, such as IoT devices and vehicles.
	- –
		**Aerial Handovers**: Handovers between UAVs or HAPs are critical for extending coverage. These involve managing connectivity among the mobility of aerial platforms, which may dynamically adjust flight paths.
	- –
		**Satellite Handovers**: Occurring between satellites (e.g., LEO or MEO), these are complex due to their rapid orbital speeds, exceeding 8,000 m/s for LEO satellites, necessitating frequent handovers to maintain continuous coverage.
- •
	**Inter-segment Handovers**: These involve transitions between different network layers , :
	- –
		**Terrestrial to Aerial**: Handovers from ground BSs to UAVs ensure connectivity as users move between fixed and mobile infrastructure, requiring precise coordination.
	- –
		**Terrestrial to Satellite**: These extend coverage to remote areas, managing transitions between low-latency terrestrial links and higher-latency satellite links.
	- –
		**Aerial to Satellite**: Handovers between UAVs and satellites must account for the independent mobility of both entities, ensuring uninterrupted service despite relative motion.

#### 2.2.2. Challenges in 6G handovers

The unique architecture of 6G networks introduces several challenges for handover management:

- •
	**High Mobility and Dynamic Topologies**: LEO satellites and UAVs exhibit rapid movement, resulting in constantly shifting network topologies. For instance, LEO satellites require handovers every few seconds due to short visibility windows.
- •
	**Heterogeneous Network Characteristics**: Differences in latency, bandwidth, and coverage between segments (e.g., satellite vs. terrestrial) complicate seamless transitions, impacting QoS.
- •
	**Energy and Resource Constraints**: UAVs, with limited battery life, must optimize handover processes to conserve energy while maintaining connectivity.
- •
	**Scalability and Coordination**: Managing handovers across thousands of satellites, UAVs, and BSs requires efficient, scalable algorithms to minimize signaling overhead.
- •
	**Predictive Handover Management**: Accurate prediction of handover needs is essential, particularly for predictable patterns like satellite orbits, but remains challenging for dynamic user mobility.

#### 2.2.3. Innovative approaches to handover management

To tackle these challenges, 6G networks leverage advanced technologies and strategies:

- •
	**AI and Machine Learning**: Predictive algorithms, such as reinforcement learning, analyze user mobility, satellite orbits, and UAV trajectories to anticipate handover needs, reducing latency and optimizing targets.
- •
	**Software-Defined Networking (SDN)**: SDN provides centralized control and dynamic resource allocation, enabling flexible handover decisions across heterogeneous segments.
- •
	**Multi-Connectivity**: Devices maintain simultaneous connections to multiple segments (e.g., terrestrial and aerial), facilitating smoother transitions without service interruption.
- •
	**Edge Computing**: Localized processing at the network edge (e.g., on UAVs) reduces latency in handover decisions, enhancing responsiveness.
- •
	**Context-Aware Protocols**: These consider user preferences, application needs, and network conditions to tailor handover strategies, improving QoS.

#### 2.2.4. Role of standardization

Standardization bodies like 3GPP are pivotal in defining 6G handover procedures. Releases beyond 17 are expected to enhance mobility management for NTNs, ensuring interoperability and consistency across space, air, and ground segments , .

#### 2.2.5. Criteria for inter-satellites handovers

MEO and LEO satellites, characterized by their rapid movement and dynamic beamforming, pose unique challenges in ensuring seamless handovers for users with high mobility, compromising ground and air entities. Challenges include maintaining connectivity during inter-satellite transitions, adapting to varying quality of service (QoS) requirements, predictive handover strategies for moving/flying users, robust security and authentication during handovers, accommodating diverse user terminal mobility scenarios, and efficient satellite coordination within a constellation. Overcoming these challenges is essential for realizing the promise of 6G networks, especially in scenarios where satellite connectivity plays a critical role, to offer uninterrupted, high-quality user experiences (QoE) in an increasingly mobile and connected world . There are four main satellite features affecting satellite selection and handover decisions, which are:

- •
	**Received Signal Strength (RSS)** of the visible satellites at user’s location. As MEO and LEO satellite constellations orbit the earth, the count of visible satellites at the user’s location undergoes dynamic changes due to their movements. Consequently, the user encounters time-dependent RSS values from this evolving set of visible satellites .
- •
	**Elevation Angle (EA)** of the satellites within the user’s visibility. EA is characterized as the geometric angle between the satellite and user positions. The minimum EA marks the initiation of the satellite visibility at the user’s location, while the maximum EA corresponds to the shortest distance between their respective locations.
- •
	**Available Bandwidth (BW)** of the visible satellites at the user’s location. Typically, the satellites in the user’s visibility have different available BW values based on their current loads. This available BW is dynamically changing as the satellite moves in its orbit.
- •
	**Remaining Visible Time (RVT)** of the satellites within user’s visibility. In general, satellite visible time is defined as the period between the initial appearance of the satellite to the user till it diminishes its visibility. This time equals twice the time the satellite needs to reach its maximum EA. Thus, RVT is the time difference between the total satellite’s visible time and its current time at the user location .

Traditional satellite selection and handover techniques are based on maximizing one of these features, i.e., maximum received power (MRP), maximum elevation angle (MEA), maximum available bandwidth (MBW), and maximum remaining visible time (MRVT). Also, some combinatorial optimization techniques are based on maximizing the combinations of more than one of these features.

#### 2.2.6. Handovers protocols based on 5G-NR network architecture

The 5G-new radio (5G-NR) network architecture developed by the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) largely resembles its predecessor, the 4G network architecture. It comprises UE, 5G gNodeB (gNB), and 5G core network (5G-CN), with the 5G-CN handling user plane function (UPF) for user data management and core access and mobility management function (AMF) for controlling user access between UE and gNB. Additionally, the 5G-CN specifies various network functions (NFs) using network function virtualization (NFV) to support diverse 5G use cases. The 5G-NR protocol stack primarily relies on CP/DP splitting, separating control and user data protocol stacks for efficient data transmission. 5G-NR supports both standalone and non-standalone deployments, allowing independent operation or integration with the existing 4G core network. However, the unprecedented mobility and complex handovers associated with the 3D unified 6G networks necessitate a reassessment of the conventional network architecture and protocol stack strategies adopted in 5G-NR and its predecessors .

To comprehend the complexity involved in managing handover procedures in the 3D unified 6G network, we should first delve into the intricacies of handling mobility issues within the 5G-NR system. The 3GPP mainly split the radio access network (RAN) functions within gNB running in the NTNs into central unit (CU) and distributed unit (DU). Additionally, the gNB-CU-control plane (gNB-CU-CP) and the core AMF are two main NFs involving mobility management functions (MMFs). The gNB-CU-CP hosts the radio resource control (RRC) for handover control and decisions, the Xn application protocol (Xn-AP) for mobility management signaling on the Xn interface (i.e., the interface between next-generation RANs \[NG-RANs\]), and the NG application protocol (NG-AP) for mobility management signaling on the NG interface (i.e., the interface between NG-RAN and the core network). The AMF hosts the NG-AP and the non-access stratum mobility management (NAS-MM) for registration and tracking.

#### 2.2.7. Practical example: Handover in a disaster relief scenario

In a post-disaster scenario, where terrestrial BSs are damaged, a UAV provides temporary coverage to a remote user. Initially connected to a terrestrial BS, the user hands over to the UAV as they move out of range. Later, as the UAV nears its energy limit, the connection transitions to an LEO satellite. This sequence involves terrestrial-to-aerial and aerial-to-satellite handovers, coordinated using AI-driven predictions and multi-connectivity to ensure seamless data relay to a distant ground station.

### 2.3. Aerial trajectory planning

Unlike satellites that follow predetermined orbits, aerial platforms, including UAVs, HAPs, and aircraft, offer greater flexibility in trajectory planning. This flexibility is crucial in addressing the mobility challenges of the 3D unified 6G network. Efficient aerial trajectory planning (ATP) involves designing optimal flight paths for these aerial platforms to ensure seamless communication, data collection, and coordination across different network layers. Numerous factors influence the effectiveness of ATP, including aerial altitude, hovering and flying speeds, expected satellite coverage, and the distribution of UEs and their traffic patterns. To optimize ATP, it is essential to consider these factors holistically, considering the distinct characteristics of each aerial platform and the dynamic nature of the network environment. Researchers are exploring energy-aware ATP techniques to address the challenge of limited aerial and UE battery capacities. These techniques maximize the collected information while minimizing energy consumption, which is critical for time-critical applications such as disaster relief. In scenarios where the exact distribution and traffic demands of UEs are unknown, AI techniques can play a pivotal role in enabling energy-efficient ATP without prior information .

The complexity of ATP further increases when multiple aerial platforms are deployed concurrently. In this multi-ATP setup, collision avoidance becomes a paramount concern, ensuring that aerial platforms do not physically collide or occupy the same area simultaneously. Employing sophisticated sensor systems and AI-based techniques can effectively prevent collisions and optimize joint multi-ATP schemes, maximizing network efficiency while ensuring the safety of aerial operations. Additionally, scheduling aerial battery recharging becomes an integral component of multi-ATP planning, ensuring the uninterrupted operation of the network. These advancements in ATP are paving the way for the efficient utilization of aerial platforms in 3D unified 6G networks, enabling seamless connectivity and data management across diverse network environments. As 6G networks continue to evolve, ATP will play an increasingly critical role in optimizing resource allocation, minimizing energy consumption, and ensuring the resilience and adaptability of future wireless networks .

### 2.4. Dynamic 3D routing

Routing algorithms ensure efficient and reliable data transmission in the three layers. These algorithms identify the optimal end-to-end (E2E) path for data packets while minimizing transmission delays and ensuring successful delivery. Due to the unique characteristics of the 3D unified 6G network, such as high mobility, dynamic network topology, heterogeneous traffic patterns, and limited power and processing capabilities, traditional stationary or quasi-stationary routing approaches may not be sufficient. Specialized highly dynamic 3D routing algorithms must be developed to address these challenges and optimize network resource utilization . One major challenge in 3D routing is the presence of inter-satellite links (ISLs) in non-geostationary orbit (NGEO) satellite networks. ISLs introduce additional complexities due to their dynamic nature and high latency. 3D routing algorithms must factor in these complexities to determine the most efficient time-dependent path for data packets traversing terrestrial and air-space segments. Another challenge is differentiating traffic flows based on their QoS requirements . Different types of traffic, such as voice, video, and data, have varying latency and bandwidth demands. 3D routing algorithms must intelligently distribute traffic across space-ground, air-ground, and space-air links to ensure that each traffic type receives the appropriate level of service. Developing effective dynamic 3D routing algorithms is essential for maximizing network performance and ensuring its successful operation. These algorithms must address the unique challenges the integrated space-air-ground environment poses, enabling efficient data transmission and supporting a wide range of applications.

Dynamic routing algorithms in NGEO satellite networks can be broadly categorized into two categories, namely constellation periodicity-based and onboard routing algorithms .

- •
	**Constellation Periodicity-based Dynamic Routing Algorithms**: These algorithms leverage the predictable and periodic nature of NGEO satellite constellation topologies to make routing decisions. They typically employ pre-computed routing tables updated periodically based on the constellation’s orbital parameters. This approach is computationally efficient and scalable, not requiring extensive real-time network updates. Examples are :
	- –
		Minimum-hop routing: This algorithm selects the path with the fewest hops between the source and destination satellites.
	- –
		Latency-based routing: This algorithm selects the path with the lowest latency, minimizing the time it takes for data packets to reach their destination.
	- –
		Link-load balancing routing: This algorithm distributes traffic across different paths to prevent congestion and improve network performance.
- •
	**Onboard Dynamic Routing Algorithms**: These algorithms perform routing decisions in real-time, adapting to the dynamic topology of the NGEO satellite network. They typically utilize time-dependent network state information, such as link availability, latency, and traffic load, to make routing decisions. This approach offers greater flexibility and adaptability to network changes but can be more computationally demanding. Examples are:
	- –
		Adaptive priority routing: This algorithm assigns priorities to paths based on their current state and traffic load, dynamically adjusting routing decisions to optimize network performance.
	- –
		Learning-based routing: This algorithm utilizes machine learning (ML) techniques to learn from network traffic patterns and make informed routing decisions.
	- –
		Model-based routing: This algorithm employs mathematical models of the NGEO satellite network to predict future network conditions and make proactive routing decisions.

Dynamic 3D routing algorithms can be categorized based on the class of traffic into three main types: delay-sensitive traffic, throughput-sensitive traffic, and best-effort traffic. **Delay-sensitive traffic** requires low latency and reliable delivery, such as voice, video, and real-time data transmissions. Routing algorithms for delay-sensitive traffic must prioritize paths with minimal latency and high link availability. **Throughput-sensitive traffic** requires high data rates and efficient transmission, such as large file transfers and bulk data streaming. Routing algorithms for throughput-sensitive traffic must prioritize paths with high bandwidth and low congestion. **Best-effort traffic** does not have strict latency or throughput requirements, such as email, messaging, and non-real-time data transfers. Routing algorithms for best-effort traffic can utilize various path selection strategies, often prioritizing factors such as overall network efficiency and resource utilization.

## 3\. Case study of joint energy aware UAV trajectory planning and UAV-centric LEO-UAV handover

Herein, we present a case study of joint energy-aware UTP and LEO satellite selection/handover for post-disaster relief scenarios. In this scenario, the nearby GBSs are completely damaged, and there will be no coverage of the survivors. Thus, a UAV is deployed to collect data from survivors’ UEs while minimizing its energy consumption. Then, it will relay this information to LEO satellites at each UE location visited, where LEO satellites will relay it to the nearest survivor GBS. The system model under investigation is comprehensively illustrated in , representing a post-disaster scenario wherein a UAV is deployed to autonomously gather situational data from multiple disjoint post-disaster zones, denoted as Z1 through Z7. Each of these zones encompasses a varying number of survivor-associated ground terminals, which may include smartphones, emergency beacons, or IoT devices transmitting critical data. The UAV operates under the constraint of incomplete environmental knowledge—it has no prior information regarding the number of active ground devices or their heterogeneous data demands. Moreover, traditional terrestrial communication infrastructure, such as nearby GBSs, is assumed to be either inoperative or severely compromised due to disaster-induced damage. To address these challenges, the UAV must dynamically plan an energy-efficient trajectory that maximizes cumulative data acquisition within its finite battery capacity. A key component of the model involves leveraging non-terrestrial communication via LEO satellites to offload collected data. However, the UAV’s awareness of LEO satellite resources is non-global and evolves over time; it only has knowledge of those satellites previously encountered during its flight. Additionally, due to the high orbital mobility of LEO satellites, the set of satellites visible from the UAV’s current location (e.g., LEO-Sat 1 through LEO-Sat 6 in varies temporally and spatially. To illustrate, consider a scenario where the UAV enters Z1, which contains a high density of survivor devices with urgent data traffic. At this location, only 4 LEO satellites — LEO-Sat 1, 2, 3 and 4 — are within line-of-sight. The UAV must rapidly evaluate their respective characteristics, such as elevation angles and instantaneous bandwidth availability, to select the most suitable satellite link. As the UAV progresses towards Z2, Z3, etc., the visibility profile of LEO satellites changes due to both UAV mobility and satellite orbits. Hence, a handover may be required to maintain a high-throughput link. This joint strategy — comprising energy-aware UTP and adaptive LEO satellite selection/handover — enables the UAV to maintain seamless connectivity, maximize data offloading, and extend operational longevity. Overall, the model addresses key challenges in dynamic post-disaster environments, such as uncertainty in ground terminal distribution, temporal variability in satellite visibility, and the need for real-time adaptive decision-making. The framework exemplifies how intelligent UAV operation can ensure robust and scalable data collection when terrestrial infrastructure is unreliable or absent. The inherently dynamic and uncertain nature of UAV-assisted disaster relief operations necessitates the deployment of adaptive and intelligent decision-making frameworks capable of operating under incomplete information. In this context, online learning emerges as a powerful tool, offering the ability to learn and adapt in real time. Among these, the multi-armed bandit (MAB) framework is particularly well-suited for such sequential decision-making tasks, where prior knowledge of the environment is unavailable or unreliable. In classical MAB settings, an agent referred to as the player seeks to maximize cumulative rewards over time by iteratively selecting from a set of actions known as arms. Through trial-and-error interaction with the environment, the player incrementally learns the mapping between arms and expected rewards, enabling increasingly optimal action selections. In more complex scenarios where arm selections incur varying costs, the objective shifts to balancing reward maximization with cost minimization, leading to the formulation of budget-constrained MAB (BC-MAB) problems. Furthermore, to enable more informed decision-making, context-aware MAB (CMAB) models incorporate side information — referred to as contexts — which may include real-time observations or historical data relevant to the arms. This allows the player to tailor its choices based on the specific characteristics of the environment at each decision epoch. To address the joint problem of energy-aware UTP and dynamic LEO satellite selection and handover, a two-phase MAB-based framework is proposed in this study. In the first phase, the UAV’s trajectory is modeled as a BC-MAB problem, where the UAV acts as the player, candidate ground zones (e.g., Z1 to Z7) are treated as arms, and the quantity of data collected from survivor devices in each zone serves as the reward signal. The energy required to reach each zone represents the associated cost. The UAV must judiciously select its next visitation zone to maximize data collection while adhering to its limited energy budget. To operationalize this phase, the budget-constrained upper confidence bound (BC-UCB) algorithm is employed, which balances exploration and exploitation under energy constraints. In the second phase, the satellite selection and handover process are formulated as a contextual MAB with dynamic arms (CMAB-DA) problem. At each selected ground zone, the UAV must choose among a time-varying set of visible LEO satellites (the dynamic arms), aiming to optimize the LEO-UAV link quality. The reward in this phase corresponds to the achievable data rate of the LEO-UAV link, while contexts include temporally evolving satellite attributes such as elevation angle, remaining visible time, and available bandwidth. The LinUCB-DA algorithm is employed to incorporate these contextual features into the decision-making process, enabling the UAV to dynamically adapt its handover strategy in response to environmental changes. By decomposing the problem into these two interconnected MAB phases, the proposed approach offers a scalable, real-time learning solution that enables the UAV to autonomously (i) manage its energy consumption through intelligent zone selection and (ii) maintain high-throughput, low-interruption satellite links through context-aware handover decisions. This framework effectively addresses key challenges in disaster relief missions, including non-stationary environments, partial observability, and operational constraints, thereby ensuring robust, uninterrupted data collection in highly dynamic post-disaster scenarios.

## 4\. Opportunities and future directions

Herein, opportunities and future research directions of the 3D unified 6G network will be presented, including the need for a new network architecture not just based on upgrading the current 5G-NR, the adoption of the promising mobility-aware transmissions like the orthogonal time frequency space (OTFS) modulation scheme, and proactive and secure handover approaches , .

### 4.1. New architecture with distributed over-segments mobility management

- •
	**For CN Architecture:** Introducing various flying RANs with different traffic needs, radio bearers, measurements, and specifications necessitates novel decentralized and unified network architectures. In this context, investigating distributed AMFs (DAMFs) within the network layers and not only in the main CN may provide an acceptable strategy. For example, there should be a dedicated S-AMF for the space segment to control its intra-handovers and inter-handovers among other network layers’ entities connected to it. Thus, different network layers can achieve ultra-low latency access and mobility management. By the same reasoning, distributed UPF (DUPF) should also be designed across the layers, as each layer has its specifications, radio bearers, and resource allocations. Thus, a revolutionary vision of distributed CN (DCN), including DAMF, DUPF, and distributed NFV (DNFV) should be designed. Meanwhile, these distributed CNs should be unified through the main CN in the ground network, which will orchestrate these distributed CNs. This can be enabled using software-defined radio (SDR) and mobile edge computing (MEC). In this way, each layer can locally manage its radio resources, handovers, beamforming, etc., highly reducing the signaling overhead and time consumption over just building over 5G-NR architecture with one centralized AMF and UPF.
- •
	**For Protocol Stacks:** As the network will contain three different segments with unique specifications and mobility requirements, protocol stacks based on one level of CD/UD plan splitting specified in 5G-NR will not be enough. Based on the vision of DCNs, DAMFs, and DUPFs stated above, protocol stacks based on multi-level CD/UD plane splitting should be investigated. Despite the conventional vision of UE and gNB in 5G-NR, in 6G, one entity can simultaneously act as a UE and gNB. For example, a UAV in the air segment will be a gNB for the UEs, while it acts as a UE for LEO satellites at the same time. This necessitates a level of CD/UD plane protocol stacks between the space and the air segments different from that between the air and ground segments, etc. Each layer will play these protocol stacks over the stated DAMF and DUPF. In this way, the latency of the overall 3D network will be highly reduced compared to being controlled with one CN with one level of CD/UD plane splitting as given in 5G-NR.
- •
	**For Backward Compatibility**, the future 6G network should provide both standalone and non-standalone deployments. Interfaces between 6G main CN and 5G-CN or 4G-CN should be maintained for non-standalone deployment. This can be managed through DAMF entities distributed in the 6G-DCNs.
- •
	**Alignment with ITU Standards** The ITU-R Recommendation M.2083-1 outlines the framework and key capabilities of IMT-2020 and beyond, emphasizing support for extreme mobility, high reliability, and the integration of NTNs. The recent ITU-R M.2160-1 (IMT-2030 Framework) provides detailed insight into the architectural requirements and use cases envisioned for 6G, including multi-layered connectivity across space, air, and ground segments. The proposed concepts, such as deploying distributed AMF and UPF entities (DAMF/DUPF), dynamic control/user plane splitting, and proactive handover strategies, are fully compatible with these ITU frameworks. Furthermore, the ITU-T Focus Group on IMT-2030 has stressed the importance of flexible, service-based core networks capable of supporting emerging verticals and NTN scenarios, which our proposed distributed core network vision directly supports. Future iterations of this work will include an explicit mapping between our proposed system components and the architectural elements defined in the IMT-2030 vision to support global standardization and interoperability better.

### 4.2. Mobility-aware OTFS signaling

OTFS is a new modulation and signaling scheme that has the potential to revolutionize wireless communication. Unlike conventional modulation schemes such as orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM), which transmits information across individual sub-carriers, OTFS spreads the information across both time and frequency domains, effectively creating a 3D signal representation called the delay-Doppler domain , . This unique approach offers several advantages over traditional modulation techniques in the 3D unified 6G network context. Firstly, OTFS can help the network achieve its higher spectral efficiency requirements by exploiting the full degrees of freedom available in the delay-Doppler domain, which in turn allows for more efficient multiplexing of multiple data streams from different segments within the same bandwidth, leading to increased data rates. Secondly, OTFS is more resilient to channel impairments suffered in the highly dynamic environment around the NTN platforms, such as multipath fading and Doppler shift, thanks to its ability to spread the signals across a wider range of delays and Doppler frequencies, and accordingly combat these challenges more effectively and maintain reliable data transmission even in challenging and highly dynamic environments extending to space, air, and ground segments . Thirdly, OTFS does not require cyclic prefixes (CPs), typically employed in conventional modulation schemes to mitigate inter-symbol interference (ISI). The absence of CPs in OTFS leads to lower latency, making it a suitable candidate for applications demanding real-time data transmission. Fourthly, OTFS offers enhanced data security as compared to traditional modulation techniques. Due to its inherent 3D signal representation, OTFS is more resistant to eavesdropping and jamming attempts expected with the 3D unified 6G network. Lastly, OTFS’s ability to achieve high spectral efficiency, robustness to channel impairments, and reduced latency makes it a strong candidate for supporting high-speed data transmission applications in future 6G networks .

While OTFS offers significant advantages over traditional modulation schemes when applied in the 3D unified 6G network, it also presents some challenges that should be addressed . OTFS signal processing is more complex than OFDM due to its 3D nature. This complexity may hinder its practical implementation in NTN platforms that mostly have limited resources. Additionally, accurate channel estimation and equalization are crucial for OTFS to achieve its full potential in highly dynamic environments with high mobility. Accurate channel knowledge is essential to properly spread the information across the delay-Doppler domain, while efficient equalization is needed to recover the distorted signal after it passes through the channel. Also, OTFS may require specialized hardware for its implementation, particularly in high-speed applications, which may be challenging with limited-resource NTNs , . This can increase the overall cost and complexity of an OTFS-based 3D unified 6G network. Despite these challenges, OTFS is a promising technology with the potential to revolutionize wireless communication through the 3D unified 6G network. Ongoing research addresses the implementation challenges and explores new applications for OTFS, paving the way for its widespread adoption.

### 4.3. Robust, proactive, and secure handover approaches

Current 5G networks rely on threshold-based handover triggers that may not be optimal for all scenarios, leading to unnecessary handovers or delays in handover initiation. 6G networks should adopt more sophisticated handover decision-making algorithms that consider factors such as link quality, user preferences, and network resource availability. Additionally, predictive handover schemes would anticipate upcoming handover events based on real-time network information and user movement patterns, enabling proactive handover initiation to minimize latency and packet loss. This is particularly important for high-speed applications and scenarios where mobility patterns are predictable. Also, 6G networks should seamlessly integrate mobility across multiple network layers, including terrestrial, aerial, and satellite networks. This involves developing inter-layer handover protocols that handle handovers between different access technologies and consider the unique characteristics of each layer.

With the introduction of moving cellular BSs and aerial platforms, 6G networks need to optimize mobility management for these dynamic network elements. This includes efficient handover procedures, resource allocation strategies, and coordination mechanisms between fixed and moving networks. 6G networks should adapt handover strategies based on real-time network conditions and user requirements. This involves dynamic handover parameters, flexible handover thresholds, and context-aware handover decision-making. ML algorithms can be leveraged to analyze large volumes of network data and user behavior to optimize handover performance. This includes predicting handover events, selecting optimal handover targets, and adapting handover parameters based on network conditions and user preferences. A final issue is that handovers introduce additional security challenges, as sensitive user information needs to be transferred between different network entities. 6G networks should employ robust security mechanisms to protect user privacy and ensure data integrity during handovers.

## 5\. Future directions

### 5.1. Standard-compliant mobility architectures

Future research should align the proposed mobility management architectures with emerging global standards such as the ITU-R IMT-2030 framework and ongoing 3GPP efforts in NTNs. Investigating scalable, service-based, and interoperable mobility frameworks will be critical to ensure global harmonization.

### 5.2. AI-driven proactive mobility management

The integration of ML and RL in handover prediction, satellite selection, and aerial path optimization remains a rich area for research. Designing lightweight and online learning models suitable for dynamic UAV and satellite environments can significantly enhance network resilience and efficiency.

### 5.3. Cross-segment context-aware protocol stack

A promising research direction involves developing context-aware protocol stacks that adapt based on the unique characteristics of each segment (space, air, ground). This includes dynamic CP/UP (control/user plane) splitting across layers and unified signaling across heterogeneous domains.

### 5.4. Mobility-aware modulation techniques

Advanced waveform design, such as OTFS modulation, is another fertile area of study. These techniques are expected to better cope with high-mobility environments by providing robustness against Doppler shifts and delay spreads.

### 5.6. Information-centric mobility models

Inspired by Named Data Networking (NDN), future work can explore producer mobility and data-centric mobility control in 6G. This includes leveraging recent models that shift from device- or user-based mobility to content-based mobility, improving caching, traffic offloading, and QoS in dense networks.

### 5.7. Multi-objective optimization in NTNs

In complex and dynamic 6G environments, especially within NTNs, network entities such as satellites, UAVs, and HAPs must simultaneously meet multiple performance criteria—e.g., maximizing throughput, minimizing latency, reducing energy consumption, and ensuring link reliability. Therefore, multi-objective optimization (MOO) frameworks are essential for efficient decision-making in such scenarios . Future research should explore adaptive and scalable MOO techniques to support joint trajectory planning, resource allocation, satellite selection, and beamforming. Leveraging evolutionary algorithms, Pareto-optimal front exploration, and machine learning-based heuristics can provide viable trade-offs between conflicting objectives. Moreover, context-aware MOO models incorporating environmental dynamics (e.g., atmospheric conditions, mobility patterns) and application-specific constraints (e.g., delay tolerance in disaster response vs. ultra-reliable low-latency in industrial use cases) are critical for enabling robust NTN operations. Integrating MOO with real-time learning and predictive analytics will further enhance the autonomy and adaptability of future NTN-driven mobility management frameworks.

## 6\. Conclusion

This paper delves into the complexities of mobility management in the emerging 3D unified 6G networks, encompassing all three layers: space, air, and ground. We explore a wide spectrum of mobility management-related techniques, including time-varying channel estimation and dynamic radio resource management, handovers within and between layers, aerial trajectory planning, and dynamic 3D routing. Furthermore, we identify promising opportunities and future directions for addressing 6G mobility management, focusing on core network architecture and protocol stacks, mobility-aware modulation schemes, and the need for proactive and secure handover approaches. Furthermore, we present a practical case study of energy-aware joint UAV trajectory planning and LEO satellite selection/handover in 3D unified 6G networks, complemented by a dedicated section on future research directions. This case study demonstrates the effectiveness of employing online learning algorithms, specifically online learning MAB models, to optimize this highly dynamic mobility management problem. The proposed approach achieves a remarkable performance of approximately 97.1% of the optimal LEO satellite handover strategy, highlighting the potential of online learning for the management of 6G mobility.
